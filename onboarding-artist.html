Artist Onboarding

Day one, from nothing. The spec you draw against, the pipeline that processes what you draw, the one rig that will bite you if nobody explains it, and exactly what to hand over. Everything here was read off the actual assets and code — where a number appears, its source is named.

01The spec — read this before you draw a pixel

Gleamwood is a 2D pixel-art game with a fixed pixel density. Almost everything an artist can get wrong here is a scale mistake, and a scale mistake is invisible in your art program and obvious in the game.

Pixels-per-unit is 64. It is not a preference, it is the project's unit of measurement. One world unit = 64 texels of your art. Every world-space character sheet the game renders is at 64: all 33 enemy/boss sheets in Assets/_Sprites/Gleamwood/Enemies/, all 15 player sheets in Assets/_Sprites/Placeholder/Player/, and every processed sheet in Assets/_Sprites/Generated/Player/. The one file in those folders at another value is REF_Archer_Idle.png, a reference image nothing renders.
What you are replacing is a placeholder, and it is scheduled to leave the repo. The 33 enemy and boss sheets under Assets/_Sprites/Gleamwood/Enemies/ (nine art sets) and the 22 attack sheets under Assets/_Sprites/Generated/Player/ are machine-generated stand-ins — the pipeline in §04 exists precisely to cut generator output, and its raws are committed under Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/. The plan is to strip them rather than to draw over them: they are removed and every slot they filled is refilled by a generated, licence-free kit of flat silhouettes and greybox tiles, produced by an editor tool from a written spec — and that generator is the contract a real sheet must meet (cell grid, PPU 64, frame counts per clip, the split rig's twin slots). It exists as of 2026-08-19: Gleamwood ▸ Dev ▸ Art ▸ Generate Placeholder Kit writes ten enemy sheets and the hero rig, and … ▸ Generate Placeholder Kit Stills writes the portraits, weapon icons and socket cap, all under Assets/_Sprites/Kit/. Run it and open a sheet — that is your spec. The kit is not what the game plays, and will not be. It was wired into every character on 2026-08-19 to prove it drops in cleanly — the suite went green on it — and pointed straight back, because flat shapes are a specification and the game looked worse for it. The stand-ins keep playing until your sheets replace them, and the strip happens then. So you are not racing a deletion: match the kit, hand it over, and the swap is one reference per renderer. Rows are clips, columns are frames, cells are 64×64 at PPU 64 pivoted BottomCenter, and each row’s frame count is exactly what the game expects, because the generator copied it off the shipped set rather than inventing it. Match a row and your sheet drops in. Draw against the contract, not against the current sheets, whose slice styles vary because they were cut by eye. Which classes go, and whether the one art tileset in the repo (Assets/_Sprites/Placeholder/Level/Emberfall_Platform.png, provenance unrecorded; no shipped room is painted with it — since 2026-08-18 it reaches play through exactly one asset, the SocketCap_Emberfall sprite. Emberfall_StandIn.prefab, the no-pool fallback that was its only other reader and that no biome could reach, is deleted) stays as the biome-look seam's worked example, is a decision recorded as OUTSTANDING "What placeholder art gets stripped, and what replaces it"; the kit itself is OUTSTANDING "The placeholder and machine-generated art has no licence-clean replacement". Until both ship, the numbers on this page describe the stand-ins.

Pivots — feet on the floor, centred

A sprite's pivot is where the game thinks the character is. Get it wrong and the character floats or sinks, and nothing logs an error. Every shipped enemy frame is horizontally centred — pivot x = 0.5 on all 272 sliced frames across all 33 sheets, without exception. Vertically the rule holds on the sheets you can rely on and not on the ones you cannot: every idle and death frame sits within four texels of the bottom of its rect, while attack sheets are pivoted wherever the pose's feet actually are — cinderhound-attack_6 is 18 texels up, pyreregent-attack_6 24, and pyrearcher-attack_11 is 50 texels up an 81-texel rect. Aim for feet-on-the-bottom; if a leaping frame's anchor is somewhere else, say where (§09). The retained legacy player sheet archer-Sheet.png uses Bottom-Center, pivot (0.5, 0), every frame. The assigned layered player instead takes its canvas and pixel pivot from its .heroart package (§02).

Bottom-centre is part of the legacy player's split-sheet contract. The waist cut is computed in object space, which on a SpriteRenderer is anchored at the pivot. With a bottom-centre pivot, "height above the pivot" is "height above the feet", so one number cuts every frame in the same place. Move the pivot and the cut moves with it. See §06.

Three pivot styles are legal and all three ship:

  • Uniform grid, pivot at the bottom of the cell. Draw all frames on identical cells, feet resting on a constant baseline. The Demon King (181 × 220, pivot 0.5/0) and the Imp (63 × 61, pivot 0.5/0) are built this way. Prefer this. It is the only style a modder can address with sprite = sheet.png 8x3, and the only one the Clip Set Builder can slice for you.
  • Tight per-frame rects with a custom pivot. Every frame cropped to its own ink, each carrying its own pivot fraction. The Cinder Hound and the Pyre Archer are like this. It packs tighter but every frame has to be pivoted by hand, and a mod sheet cannot reproduce it.
  • Tight per-frame rects, one shared Bottom-Center pivot. The middle case, and what fourteen of the fifteen legacy player sheets do: rects vary frame to frame (archer-Sheet.png's 46 frames span 67–95 wide by 77–96 tall, 29 distinct sizes) but every frame is Bottom-Center at (0.5, 0), because the art is drawn feet-down on a common baseline. Nothing is pivoted by hand, and it is what §06's split rig requires. T_DaggerDashAtk is the one legacy player sheet that is not.

Read the pivot values, not the alignment enum — they disagree. Every enemy sheet is alignment: 9 (Custom) except the Demon King's nine at 7, yet the Imp's Custom pivots are all exactly (0.5, 0) on a uniform 63 × 61 grid, which makes it a grid sheet in everything but the enum.

Backgrounds: transparent, except in one place

Everything you deliver: real alpha

Anything that goes into Assets/_Sprites/ or a mod's sprites/ folder ships with a genuine transparent background — the importer runs alphaIsTransparency: 1 and the shader multiplies against it. No matte colour, no white halo, no semi-transparent fringe from a bilinear resize.

Generator raws: opaque magenta

The only keyed-background art in the project is the untouched generator output kept in Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/. Image generators do not emit alpha — they paint a flat magenta field — so those files are opaque RGB and the pipeline (§04) keys them. If you are drawing by hand, you never produce one of these.

One consequence worth knowing: a magenta key is not free. process_boss_strip.py records that the border ring of one generated sheet held 92 distinct colours dithering around ≈#F70B8E, which is why the key is now the statistical mode of the whole ring rather than a corner sample. Real alpha has none of these problems.

Editing an authored drawing. The package supplies layer names, native canvas, pivot, pixel scale, atlas tiles and their registration. Keep these coordinates when painting a replacement; a waist or weapon socket depends on the selected pose and its authored attachment.

  1. Select the action, equipment, timing and individual layer in Presentation. Use Export selected layer PNG + registration to save its source RGBA and registration sidecar.
  2. Edit the PNG at its original canvas size. Use Import edited layer into new review package, select the sidecar and edited PNG, and choose a new package name. The viewer creates a new folder and opens the resulting package; the shipped package stays unchanged.
  3. Review the complete sprite and every context that reuses the drawing, including independent clock combinations. Hidden body artwork must be authored where a weapon replacement exposes it; separating ownership alone does not supply those pixels.

Verification and export. Verify current action and Verify definition check authored drawing and renderer integrity. The menu item Gleamwood ▸ Art ▸ Verify Selected Art verifies the current action. Reports record the selected source and hashes. Export playback writes a native PNG sheet and timing JSON at 60 Hz under Logs/ArtViewer/Playback/, including the selected inspection settings. It is capped at 512 samples, 8192 pixels per side and 64 MiB of decoded sheet pixels. Verification and selected-input playback do not establish incoming gameplay transitions or final animation quality.

The verifier refuses shared-clock products above 65,536 combinations. The existing C_Run_Bow_Action needs about 311,040 and therefore prevents a complete definition-wide pass within that budget. This is a review bound, not a runtime table limit. A selected-input playback or passing smaller action does not cover the refused combinations.

The .heroart importer and campaign loader share the gleamwood.layered-sprite.v1 format, validating the atlas and up to eight drawing layers. Campaigns select it with [PlayerSkin] layered = art/player.heroart; see the player-art grammar. Remaining animation coverage and polish are tracked in OUTSTANDING "The placeholder and machine-generated art has no licence-clean replacement". The current review evidence does not establish Steam Deck/Proton performance.

Slicing

  1. Select the PNG, set the fields above, hit Apply.
  2. Sprite Editor ▸ Slice. For a uniform sheet use Grid By Cell Size with your cell dimensions and Pivot Bottom Center. For a tight-cropped sheet use Automatic and then fix each pivot.
  3. Frames end up named <sheet>_0, <sheet>_1, … That trailing number is the frame index every later tool addresses — the Clip Set Builder sorts on it (ClipSetBuilderWindow.SpriteIndex) and so does DemonKingArtBuilder.NameIndex.
  4. Or skip 2–3 entirely: the Clip Set Builder (§05) will grid-slice a sheet for you with a pixel pivot, then build the clips in the same click.

Cells number left-to-right, then top-to-bottom. Row 0 of an 8-wide sheet is frames 0–7, row 1 is 8–15. Unity's texture origin is bottom-left, so every reader in this project explicitly flips the row (ModClipSetBuilder: int y = (art.Rows - 1 - row) * cellH;) — meaning you export the way an exporter writes, top row first, and it comes out the right way up.

04The art pipeline — Tools/ArtPipeline/

Three Python scripts that turn generator output into game-ready sheets. You do not need them for hand-drawn art — if you are exporting clean, uniform, transparent sheets from Aseprite or Photoshop, skip to §05. You need them when art arrives as an unsliced strip on a painted background, which is what an image generator produces.

Running them

# From the repo root. numpy, Pillow and scipy are required and already installed
# for the interpreter at C:/Python313/python.exe.
# build_ground_raws.py and process_aerial_sheets.py resolve their own paths off
# __file__, so cwd cannot break them. process_boss_strip.py takes every path from
# the command line and opens it as given -- so BOTH sides of #3 are repo-relative.

# 1. Rebuild the GROUND raws from art already in the game (dry run first)
python Tools/ArtPipeline/build_ground_raws.py
python Tools/ArtPipeline/build_ground_raws.py --apply

# 2. Key / cut / feet-anchor / repack every player attack sheet
python Tools/ArtPipeline/process_aerial_sheets.py
python Tools/ArtPipeline/process_aerial_sheets.py --apply

# 3. One boss strip -> one game-ready sheet
python Tools/ArtPipeline/process_boss_strip.py in.png out.png --cell-h 193 --expect 6

# 3b. SEVERAL boss strips under ONE shared scale and ONE shared cell
python Tools/ArtPipeline/process_boss_strip.py \
    idle=Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/demonking-concept/idle3-strip-RAW.png \
    walk=Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/demonking-concept/walk-strip-RAW.png \
    cast=Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/demonking-concept/cast-strip-RAW.png \
    ignite=Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/demonking-concept/ignite2-strip-RAW.png \
    slam2=Tools/ArtPipeline/raws/demonking-concept/slam2-strip-RAW.png \
    --batch Assets/_Sprites/Gleamwood/Enemies --ref idle --body-h 150 \
    --fit ignite --fit slam2:1

Every --fit name must also be one of the name=path pairs. The fit table is only consulted for sheets that were measured, so --fit ignite without an ignite= pair is a silent no-op — no warning, and the sheet that needed its own scale quietly gets the shared one. The name on the left of the = is what the output is called (demonking-<name>.png), which is why the pair is ignite=…/ignite2-strip-RAW.png and not the other way round.

Both bulk scripts are dry-run by default and write nothing until you pass --apply. Read the printed report first — it names the frame count, cell size and pivot it measured for every sheet. process_aerial_sheets.py additionally refuses the whole run and writes nothing if a direction variant does not cut to the same frame count as the neutral sheet it was derived from.

What each one does

build_ground_raws.py

In: a clip name in Player.asset and its source sheet. Out: a 1024 × 1024 magenta raw in raws/, plus ground_plan.json.

Runs backwards: it unpacks an already-shipped, already-transparent strip and lays it back out on a magenta grid, because a generator handed a transparent strip invents a background and returns frames at different sizes. It records the exact upscale it used so step 2 can invert it rather than guess.

process_aerial_sheets.py

In: raws/<Name>.png for each of the 22 sheets in its SHEETS table. Out: packed sheets into Assets/_Sprites/Generated/Player/ + aerial_cut.json.

Keys the magenta, isolates each figure by connected components, cuts merged figures at the cell wall, then feet-anchors every frame into one uniform cell so the character cannot slide or bob between frames.

process_boss_strip.py

In: an evenly-spaced pose strip on a painted background. Out: a uniform-cell sheet, feet on a common baseline.

Measures the frame grid off the image instead of trusting a requested count, and in --batch mode scales every sheet by one factor taken from one reference pose.

--fit, properly. The default rule is one scale factor for every sheet in a batch, and that rule exists because the alternative visibly breaks the character: scaling each sheet to its own tallest pose means a raised arm plus a fireball (476 px on the cast strip) normalises to the same cell height as a plain standing figure (349 px on idle), so the boss shrinks every time he casts.

The invariant actually wanted is "his body is the same height in every sheet". The shared factor is only the cheapest way to get it, and it holds only while every raw draws the character at roughly the same size — which a later generation need not. The ignite strip came back with the figure ≈1.7× larger than idle, so the shared factor left him towering over his own other animations. --fit ignite derives that one sheet's factor from its own frame 0 instead: the same rule, applied to a sheet the shared factor does not fit.

It is opt-in because frame 0 must be a plain standing body to be a valid reference, and often is not. The relocate strip's frame 0 is a bodiless cape and the death strip's is a lightning burst — fitting either scales the boss to the size of a prop. When the rest pose is not first, name it: --fit slam2:1 uses frame index 1, because that strip opens on a deep crouch and fitting a crouch to standing-body height inflates the whole sheet.

What the pipeline knows that you should too

These are recorded as comments in the scripts because each one shipped a plausible-looking, silently wrong sheet first. They are also a decent checklist for hand-drawn strips:

  • The frame count is measured, never assumed. A sheet prompted for six poses came back with five. Slicing on the assumed count shears every frame in half — and the result still imports, still animates, and reads as a rigging bug rather than a slicing one.
  • Debris is not a frame. Trailing embers and single-pixel edge noise produced spans one pixel wide; the cast sheet measured eight frames for six poses until runs carrying a trivial share of ink were dropped.
  • Poses that touch are not one pose. A greatsword reaching into the next figure's column made a five-pose walk cycle measure as a single span. The strip's repeat period is measured by autocorrelation and arbitrates — taking the smallest strong period, because a walk cycle's left and right steps are mirror images and correlate better at twice the true spacing.
  • An even cut through overlapping art leaves a sliver of the neighbour in each cell — a scrap of cape hanging in mid-air that animates as a flicker at the sprite's edge. Only the dominant connected blob survives, with a floor low enough that a legitimately detached part (the relocate strip is a body dissolving into separate embers) is kept.
  • Feet are the lowest row wide enough to be feet — max(4, 18% of the frame's widest row) — not simply the lowest ink. Blades and capes hanging below the boots were dragging the baseline down; under this rule they hang below the pivot instead of moving it.
  • Scale is calibrated on face area. Cape extent oversized the body ~1.5×, hair-blob width disagreed with itself by 1.8× across sheets, and judging by eye off a contact sheet undershot by 20–48% — which shipped, and was reported from a build as "the aerial attacks now are all smaller". Face pixel area is small, rigid and pose-invariant; linear scale = sqrt(reference_area / measured_area).
Lowering PPU is never the fix for "this sheet is the wrong size". It was the obvious move for the aerial sheets and it is wrong: those sheets would then be the only player art at a different pixel density, and mismatched pixel size is visible in pixel art. The raws are 1024 × 1024, so scaling up in the processor resamples less and keeps every player sprite at 64.

After the pipeline: getting the cut back into Unity

process_aerial_sheets.py writes PNGs and records the cell size and pivot it packed to in raws/aerial_cut.json, but nothing reads that file back automatically — the Unity-side slice is manual. Open each sheet's Sprite Editor, Grid By Cell Size with the recorded cell, pivot Custom with the recorded fraction. The values in the json match the shipped .meta exactly, so a re-run that changes a number is telling you the slice has to be redone.

// raws/aerial_cut.json, first entry — and the same numbers appear in
// Assets/_Sprites/Generated/Player/AerialSword.png.meta
{ "name": "AerialSword", "frames": 6,
  "cell": [138, 153], "pivot": [0.41304, 0.19608] }

Note that pivot x is not 0.5 there. These frames are anchored on the feet, and a swinging figure's feet are not under the centre of its bounding box.

05Animation — what a clip is, and how you write one

There is no Animator Controller graph to learn. A character's whole animation vocabulary is a plain data asset — SpriteClipSet — holding named clips, each a list of sprite frames with a rate and a loop flag. The state machine calls Play("Run"); there are no transitions, no parameters and no animation events anywhere in it.

Why it is data and not a controller: a controller cannot be authored at runtime, so a mod could never add an animation — and a missing state made Animator.Play silently no-op, freezing a character forever with no error. SpriteClipSet is ordinary serialized data, so it can be built at runtime from a sheet and a text file, which is the whole point. (SpriteClipSet.cs.)

A clip, field by field

FieldMeans
nameWhat a state config names. Matched case-insensitively.
framesSprites in order. One frame is legal — a static pose.
fpsFrames per second.
loopRepeat, or hold the last frame.
durationSeconds for one pass. 0 derives it as FrameCount / fps. Set it explicitly only when your clip genuinely ends part-way through its last frame's slice — seven player clips do, and deriving them stretched the fall loop 36% and the jump apex 21%, which retimes the transitions gated on them rather than looking obviously broken.
slotWhich renderer this clip drives, when a character has more than one. Empty = the track's default. This is the split rig — see §06.
hideThis clip hides its renderer rather than showing frames — the "nothing here right now" state. Has no frames by definition.

The grammar (this is the mod file's; the editor tool reads a subset)

A mod's .ini declares clips with the line format below, parsed by ModClipSetBuilder.ParseClipLine. The Clip Set Builder window inside Unity reads a subset of it — see the warning after the tables before you write a line there. Learn the full form once:

; Name = <frames> [fps N] [loop|once] [pivot P] [slot S] [duration D]

clip Idle  = 0-7              fps 8
clip Bite  = 16-21            fps 16 once
clip Rewind= 7-0              fps 12          ; a DESCENDING range plays backwards
clip Blink = 0,3,5            fps 10          ; an explicit list
clip Hover = hover_*.png      fps 12          ; separate files, one per frame
clip Down  = 23               once pivot 0  ; a corpse lies flat on the floor
clip Weapon= hides                           ; show nothing at all

0-7An inclusive range, ascending.
7-0The same range reversed — how you get a rewind out of a sheet drawn one way.
0,3,5Exactly those frames, in that order. Combine freely: 0-3,7,5-4.
run_*.pngA glob over separate image files, sorted by name. Zero-pad your numbers (run_00, run_01, …) — the sort is ordinal, so run_10 would otherwise land before run_2.
one.pngA single named file.
Clips loop by default — write once for attacks and deaths. The asymmetry is deliberate: a clip that loops when it should not keeps moving while the state machine still advances, whereas a clip that stops when it should not holds its last frame forever with no error. Looping is the failure you can see. A death clip is the one you will forget, and the mod loader logs every multi-frame clip that took the default — the note is gated on frames.Count > 1 (ModClipSetBuilder.cs), so a one-frame corpse or held pose is not flagged and has to be checked by hand. The Clip Set Builder window does not log this at all.

The two routes share the RANGE parser, not the grammar. ClipSetBuilderWindow calls ModClipSetBuilder.ParseIndices for the frame list — so 0-7, 7-0 and 0-3,7,5-4 mean the same thing in both places and cannot drift. Everything else is its own scanner, and it is narrower:

It used to drop slot on the floor, and that is worth knowing about. Until recently the window knew only fps/loop/once/pivot: typing Upper_Run = 0-11 fps 18 slot UpperBody built a clip with an empty slot and reported success, PlayerAnimation.RendererForSlot routed it to the default renderer, and the waist came apart with nothing on screen to explain it — exactly the failure §06 exists to prevent. The keyword set matches the mod grammar now, and a word the window cannot read is refused with the list of what it can rather than ignored. If you are reading an older doc that says to hand-author split pairs in Player.asset, that instruction is stale.

The tool: Clip Set Builder

Inside Unity, Gleamwood ▸ Dev ▸ Animation ▸ Clip Set Builder turns a sheet plus a few lines of text into a SpriteClipSet asset, instead of dragging sprites into an array one at a time.

  1. Drop your sheet into the Sheet field.
  2. If it is not sliced yet, tick "Slice the sheet to a grid first" and give it Columns, Rows, and Pivot (px above bottom) — how many pixels your character's feet sit above the bottom of a cell, usually 0. (This rewrites the sheet's slice, so leave it unticked for a sheet you sliced by hand.)
  3. Write one clip per line in the Clips box: run = 4-9 fps 12, or Upper_Run = 4-9 fps 12 slot UpperBody for a split-rig half — write the slot, or the clip drives the wrong renderer and the waist comes apart (§06). Frame ranges by index, plus fps N / loop / once / slot NAME / duration S / hide, and pivot P, which is accepted and ignored on purpose because a pivot belongs to the sliced sprites rather than to a clip. A word this window cannot read refuses that line and prints the list of what it can — see the comparison table above for how that set differs from a mod's .ini.
  4. Leave Update (optional) empty to create a new asset beside the sheet, named <sheet>_Clips.asset; or point it at an existing set to overwrite it.
  5. Build, then read the report line by line — see below.
"Built N clip(s)" is not a pass. A line that fails is skipped and the surviving lines are written anyway; the failures are demoted under a Notes: heading beneath a success header, so three clip lines with one bad frame range writes two clips and reads as a win. Count the N. Nothing is written only in three whole-run cases: the grid slice failed, the sheet has no sliced sprites, or every line failed. And because Update (optional) replaces the clip array wholesale (set.clips = clips.ToArray()), one mistyped range on an existing set deletes that clip from the asset under the same cheerful header.

One limitation worth knowing before you rely on it: its grid slicer always centres the pivot horizontally. Sheets that need an off-centre anchor — the feet-anchored aerial attacks from §04 — must be sliced in the Sprite Editor instead.

There is also right-click an AnimatorController ▸ Gleamwood ▸ Convert Animator to SpriteClipSet for legacy art. It refuses any clip carrying rotation, position, scale or float curves rather than silently flattening it to a sprite swap.

06The legacy split rig

Plain sprite skins and declared fallback contexts use the split rig described below. The player still publishes two logical animation tracks, but the assigned layered appearance composes its body parts through one root SpriteRenderer; its contract is described in "Reviewing hero animation and drawing layers" above. Both drawing methods preserve the running stride while the upper body aims or fires.

How it works

The retained legacy route uses two child SpriteRenderers, named Legs and UpperBody (those exact GameObject names are what a clip's slot matches). Both draw full-body frames; they are suppressed while the layered root is drawing. The shader they share — UniversalSpriteLit, via SliceEffect.hlsl — carries a half-plane cut:

dist = dot(localPos - _SliceOrigin, _SliceNormal);
discard where dist * _CullSide > 0;

Object space on a SpriteRenderer is anchored at the sprite's pivot, so with a bottom-centre pivot and an up normal dist is simply height above the feet. PlayerSplitRig gives the legs renderer +1 and the torso renderer −1, and the same drawing is cut in half at the waist. Both halves then share one pivot, so the feet cannot disagree — the old failure (two hand-tuned anchors in different files) stops being a bug to fix and becomes a state that cannot be represented.

The cut sits at waistLocalY = 0.625 — exactly 40/64, i.e. 40 texels above the feet, on the belt. It is on an exact texel boundary on purpose: the previous value, 0.62, is 39.68 px, two-thirds of the way into texel row 39. The discard is per-fragment on an interpolated position, and the camera smooth-damps its orthographic size during a room zoom, so at intermediate scales the top third of row 39 flipped from the legs to the torso. A row of the character changed owner depending on how far the camera had zoomed.

THE LEGACY SHEET RULE: both halves of a split pair must come from the SAME drawing. These flattened sheets were not authored as independently compatible body regions.

Measured silhouette discontinuity across the join, averaged over all twelve stride frames of the retained archer sheet:

These measurements explain the old full-body pairing contract; they do not prohibit purpose-authored layered body regions with verified attachments and ownership.

Export contracts: legacy sheets and current layers

Measured waist sockets in source plans. The offline package builder accepts an optional attachmentSockets field on a multi-axis layer table. Its target is the lower drawing's waist; its source is the upper drawing's matching seam. Measure both after source placement in the common package canvas, using integer pixels with the origin at bottom-left. For example:

"attachmentSockets": {
  "target": {"axis": "lower", "positions": [[32,24], [33,25]]},
  "source": {"axis": "upper", "positions": [[32,24], [31,24]]}
}

The builder expands target minus source into the existing runtime attachment table, independently indexed by both axes. Matching axis names instead pair equal frames for coordinated poses. The original upper/lower PNG references and clocks remain separate; the packed runtime data contains only ordinary attachment offsets. Supply either explicit attachment offsets or sockets, never both. The builder checks frame counts, integer coordinates, aggregate table limits and every reachable translated image boundary. Socket alignment translates pixels: it does not deform an incompatible waist or repair limb/weapon ownership. Compatible overlap and seam contours still belong in the art. Source: Tools/ArtPipeline/build_player_animation_package.py; 42 offline tests passed in Logs/style_c_socket_authoring_tests.txt.

Legacy fallback: one whole drawing

For a plain sprite skin or replacement legacy sheet, draw the running-with-bow cycle as one complete figure; the same frames supply both halves. The waist cut is a shared number in that contract.

Layered body regions need compatible poses

The old flattened standing and running drawings have incompatible waist deformation, lean and cape/weapon overlap. The current layered route uses deliberately compatible body regions, authored attachments and declared layer order. Selecting independent clocks still requires checking the visible join and occlusion in the resulting combinations.

Current player: separate body, cape and gear

The assigned player.heroart already carries separately registered body, cape and equipment artwork. Use Art Viewer's layer export and edited-layer import to replace a drawing within that package (§02). The retained legacy sheets still bake cape and weapons into their body pixels.

Keep the declared registration

Legacy halves share one bottom-centre pivot. Layered drawings use the package canvas, pixel pivot and per-layer placement, including authored attachment offsets. Keep an exported layer's canvas size and registration JSON while editing; do not crop the image or invent a separate torso pivot to repair a join.

Review the complete sprite as well as its layers. The shipped Player.prefab uses the layered shader on its root renderer. Legacy paired sheets remain available for plain skins and explicit fallback contexts. Flattened review sheets help judge silhouettes and transitions; the editable layered package retains each drawing's registration, clocks and equipment ownership.

Independent logical tracks preserve the stride when the upper action changes. The layered route composes those clocks on one root renderer; the legacy route uses the retained child renderers. The number of drawing renderers does not determine the number of animation clocks.

07Enemies and bosses

An enemy's art is a small, regular package: a handful of sheets, one clip set, one prefab with colliders sized in world units. The behaviour side is Enemies & Bosses; this is only the part you draw.

What a shipped enemy actually ships

Three sheets, usually. The shipped roster settled on <enemyid>-idle.png, <enemyid>-attack.png and <enemyid>-death.png, with the attack sheet carrying the whole melee chain and the run cycle. Cinder Hound is representative: its seven clips cut from three files —

A one-frame clip is fine — lunge is a single held pose. Only idle and run loop; everything else plays once. The Imp Scout is the exception that proves a fourth sheet is allowed when the motion earns it: it has a dedicated imp-run.png.

Clip names are lowercase <enemyid>-<state>, and they are load-bearing. An enemy's states name their animation by string, and EnemyAnimationWhitelist refuses to spawn a creature whose state names a clip that does not exist — with a message listing every clip it does have. So a typo is caught at the door, not found as a character that flickers oddly three rooms later. Match the shipped naming exactly unless a programmer is renaming states in the same change.

Bosses

A boss is the same package with more sheets and one extra rule. The Demon King ships nine: idle, walk, cast, attack, hit, relocate, death, ignite, slam2. All nine share one cell size (181 × 220) and one scale, because a boss that changes size when he changes state is the first thing anyone notices. That uniformity is produced by process_boss_strip.py --batch (§04) and then asserted in the editor: DemonKingArtBuilder refuses to build if any sheet slices to a different frame count than it expects, because a silently short clip animates as a stutter and reads as a timing bug rather than a slicing one.

Clip ranges are written against the sheets that exist, not the poses that were requested — the attack and cast strips were asked for six and returned five. If you deliver a strip with a different pose count than planned, say so; the clip ranges are hand-authored against your actual frames.

Colliders — what your sprite size costs

Hurtboxes and ground colliders are authored on the prefab in world units, not derived from the sprite. At 64 PPU, multiply by 64 to compare against your art:

The collider is smaller than the drawing, deliberately, and it will not follow your art. A hurtbox covers the body; capes, weapons, horns and flame trails hang outside it. If you make a creature noticeably bigger or smaller than the sheet it is replacing, the collider does not move and the fight silently retunes — hits land on empty air, or the player takes damage from a gap. Flag any size change in the handover (§09) so the prefab is retuned in the same change, and check it with F2 in play (§08).

Sorting is not something you configure per sheet, but it is not trivial either. ProjectSettings/TagManager.asset declares three sorting layers, in draw order back to front: Default, Play Area, Player. Every shipped enemy prefab renders on Play Area; the player's three renderers are on Player at orders 1–2; Default is what a fresh SpriteRenderer gets and it draws behind both. So a new character left on the default layer disappears behind the level no matter what sorting order it is given — flag it and let a programmer set the layer on the prefab. One band is reserved: Player at order 1000 is the readable band, where an interactable's floating label and its button glyph draw, and no art may go there — a prop that reaches it draws over a hint the player needs. InteractPromptSortingTests fails the suite if any serialized renderer in any scene or prefab climbs into it.

08Getting art into the game and looking at it

Two routes. One needs Unity, one does not. Both end with you looking at your own pixels moving.

Route A — in the editor

  1. Drop the PNG into the right folder (§03).
  2. Set the importer fields (§02) and slice.
  3. Gleamwood ▸ Dev ▸ Animation ▸ Clip Set Builder → point at the sheet, write your clip lines, Build (§05).
  4. Assign the resulting SpriteClipSet to the character's SpriteAnimator, or point an existing clip set's clips at your new frames.
  5. Press Play.

Weapon and bow clip names are chosen in the workbench, not in code: Gleamwood ▸ Content Workbench ▸ Ranged ▸ Presentation ▸ Animation holds the bow's draw/hold/release states, and ▸ Melee ▸ Aerials / ▸ Ground ▸ The Move holds each directional attack's clip. → Workbench

Those dropdowns read the clip set, so they list what the game actually plays. AnimatorStateRefDrawer resolves the SpriteClipSet on the rig's SpriteAnimator — Player.asset for the player, the enemy's own set for an enemy. It did not always: it read the legacy Assets/_Sprites/Placeholder/Player/Anim/Player.controller, which knows 3 of the 21 directional clip names the shipped weapons author, so 18 correct values rendered red with [MISSING] and a clip you had just authored never appeared in the list at all. A validator that flags the right answer as wrong teaches you to ignore it. If a name still shows red, it is genuinely absent from the clip set — believe it. The small [T] toggle still drops to raw text when you need it.

Route B — the mod folder, no Unity at all

A campaign is a folder next to the game carrying a mod.ini. Its art is read off disk at runtime — no import, no meta, no editor.

For plain sprites, write the clip grammar from §05 into the ini; their pixels-per-unit comes from the shipped donor, so the player's frames land at 64. For layered drawing, write layered = art/player.heroart under [PlayerSkin]; its canvas, pivot and pixels-per-unit are package data. Both declarations may share one skin, with the plain clips serving as the explicit fallback. Full guides: Modding §06 for the player, Enemies §12 for creatures.

The player skin owns appearance. It supplies drawing and clip data; it does not replace the player state graph, damage rules or projectile events. A layered-only skin keeps the donor gameplay clips. A bad layered package or an invalid declared plain fallback refuses the whole pair, leaving the shipped appearance intact. Movement tuning belongs to the separate player-feel tier.
Check the exact clip names. A misspelled plain clip is built but reported as a name the player never asks for, with a nearest-name hint. Under clips = extend, clip Idel = 0-7 never plays and the inherited Idle remains visible. The reverse case is also loud: under clips = replace, a missing requested name raises [SpriteAnimator] … has no clip … once. The generated template lists the real vocabulary.

Start from the generated template, not a blank file

Gleamwood ▸ Dev ▸ Player ▸ Generate Skin Template writes Documentation/PlayerSkinTemplate/PlayerSkin.ini listing every clip the player actually has, in two blocks (body/legs, then the slot UpperBody torso clips), each with its real frame rate and loop flag, every line commented out. It is read from the live Player.asset, so it is always current at the moment you generate it — regenerate before you use it rather than trusting the copy in the repo, which is only as fresh as the last time somebody ran the menu item. A build ships the same file at Mods/_Templates/example-campaign/Player/PlayerSkin.ini.

There is also a complete, droppable example campaign in Documentation/ExampleMod/ — a working [PlayerSkin] recolouring the archer icy blue, with one PNG per frame and a glob per clip. Regenerate it with Gleamwood ▸ Dev ▸ Player ▸ Generate Example Skin before using it as a reference; like the template, the committed copy is a snapshot.

09The handover checklist

What to deliver, in what format, named how. If every box here is ticked, your art can be in the game without a single question coming back to you.

Every delivery

  • PNG, 8-bit RGBA, real alpha. No matte colour, no flattened white, no JPEG anywhere in the chain.
  • Nearest-neighbour everywhere. If you scaled anything, it was by an integer factor with no interpolation. A single row of semi-transparent fringe is visible at 64 PPU.
  • Drawn at final size. Do not deliver a 4× sheet expecting it to be downscaled — see §01 on why lowering PPU is not the fix either.
  • Feet on a constant baseline across every frame of a clip, so the character cannot bob when the animation plays.
  • State the cell size and the pivot in texels in the handover note: "cells are 96 × 112, feet sit 2 px above the bottom of a cell". That sentence is exactly what both the Sprite Editor and a mod's pivot = line need.

Flag these explicitly — they are not visible in the file

  • A size change. "This creature is 20% taller than the one it replaces" means a prefab collider has to be retuned in the same change (§07).
  • A frame-count change. Clip ranges are hand-authored against your frames; one fewer pose silently shortens a clip.
  • Anything that crosses the player's waist — a longer cape, a slung weapon, a trailing scarf (§06).
  • Anything that should loop. The default is loop; attacks and deaths must be marked once, and a death clip is the one everyone forgets.
  • Non-centred anchors. If a frame's feet are not under the middle of its cell, say where they are in texels — the x,y pivot form exists for exactly this.
Before it is called done: import it, play it, and look at it with F2 on. A sheet that imports cleanly and animates smoothly can still be a texel off the floor or half a body wider than its hurtbox, and neither shows up anywhere but on screen.
