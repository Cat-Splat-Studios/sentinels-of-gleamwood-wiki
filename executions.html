Execution payoffs

A finisher is a list of beats on an asset, not a C# class. One ExecutionPayoffConfig holds the choreography; a scheduler places it on a timeline, applies the caps and the accessibility policy, and a single runner walks it and kills the enemy whatever the beats did. This page is the beat vocabulary, the composition rules, the traps that make a payoff render nothing, and how to watch one without earning it.

01What a payoff is, and how a kill reaches one

Three assets stand between a hit and a finisher: the definition says when it may fire, the payoff config says what it looks like, and the enemy's ordered list decides which one wins.

AssetOwnsWhere
ExecutionDefinitionThe trigger — damage type, airborne/grounded, reaction, recently-spiked, a status count — plus which config plays.Assets/_Game/Enemies/Data/ExecutionDefinitions/ (ten shipped)
ExecutionPayoffConfigThe choreography: StateName, an optional EnterSound, and the Beats list.…/ExecutionDefinitions/ExecutionConfig/*Payoff.asset (ten shipped)
EnemyDefinition.ExecutionsWhich definitions this enemy offers, in order.the enemy's own definition — see enemies.html §05

The path is short and every step of it is somewhere else on this wiki. The meter fills (combat.html §07), EnemyStateMachine.TakeHit sees it was already full before this hit landed, and it walks the enemy's Executions list asking each definition's CheckConditions. The first one that answers yes wins: the machine changes to the state registered under that config's StateName, and nothing after it is consulted.

The ORDER of Executions is behaviour, and both rules were live bugs first. TakeHit takes the first match and stops, so a definition that asks for strictly less than a later one makes that later one unreachable — silently, with no error, on every enemy that carries both.

Most specific first. The shipped pair that shows it: Skyhook asks for melee + juggled + a Spike reaction and Meteor asks for melee + juggled and nothing else, so Meteor is a strict superset — above Skyhook it would swallow every aerial melee and the Spike variant could never fire. The juggleable roster therefore lists Skyhook first and Meteor fourth. ExecutionTriggerTests.NoExecution_IsShadowedByAnEarlierOneOnTheSameEnemy refuses any such pair on every enemy, because an inspector drag reorders the list in one click.

Which of the pair is gated is a DESIGN call, and it is why the order moved. The two swapped gates on 2026-08-27, at Hisham's ask: Skyhook is the showpiece — the spike drives them into the floor hard enough to ricochet back at the lens — and a payoff whose trigger you meet constantly stops being a payoff, so the showpiece is now the earned one and the common airborne kill gets the shorter Meteor. Swapping the gates flips which definition is more specific, so it was two edits and not one: the guids also swapped position in all four juggleable enemies, or the new catch-all at index 0 would have shadowed the new specific one at index 3 and Skyhook would never have fired again.

Status-gated catch-alls last. A status is a state the player carries around rather than a thing they just did, so a status gate high in the list swallows every execution that shares its damage type — with the greatsword equipped, a Firestorm at slot 4 takes every melee finisher below it. Explosion and Firestorm are the two, and they sit at slots 9 and 10 where they mean "the execution when nothing better applied". This half is convention rather than a test.

Registration is automatic and it dedupes by StateName. EnemyStateMachine.Awake registers every config named by the Executions list — you do not add them to States — and it skips a name already registered. That dedupe is exactly how three configs once served ten executions and the player could not tell which finisher they had earned, so every payoff asset needs its own StateName. A blank one registers under "" and no trigger can reach it; the Workbench flags both cases.

All ten executions are data now, and that is the north star's own test passed. Ten definitions point at ten payoff assets with ten distinct StateNames — a campaign supplying all of them would leave nothing hand-written load-bearing. The last to convert was SamuraiSlice, whose sprite-halving was called irreducibly bespoke for as long as the SpriteSplit beat was a no-op; it turned out to need exactly one new field (Material) and no new seam. Before this, three bespoke states served all ten definitions — so Pinfall, Skyhook and SamuraiSlice were literally one state object and the player could not tell which finisher they had earned. ExecutionStateConfig stays abstract on purpose, so a hand-written payoff class remains legal forever: the data tier replaces the bespoke ones rather than closing the door behind them.

02The beat vocabulary — all nineteen kinds

One fat struct with a Kind discriminator. Every field exists on every beat and each kind reads three or four of them, so the column that matters is what it reads — a value authored anywhere else is a value nothing looks at.

Every kind also reads the placement trio — WithPrevious, StartAt and Duration — which the scheduler consumes before any beat runs (§03), and most honour Unscaled. The table below lists only the fields the beat reads on top of those. Read it as the runner's own switch, because that is where every row came from: ExecutionPayoffState.Fire, ApplyTween, ApplyMaterial, ApplyRenderer, Spawn, FireGpuExplosion, Split and Resolve.

The two resolvers

Direction answers "where does this beat point". GpuExplosion and SpriteSplit read it directly — which edge to fling the blast at, which way the cut runs — and SpawnPrefab and Translate read it indirectly, whenever their Position is ScreenEdge. It never returns zero, which is the whole reason the first option is called WithFallbacks: a Crumple reaction zeroes knockback by definition, and a slice normal derived from a zero vector cuts along no plane — that is how a sprite gets halved into two invisible pieces.

DirectionResolverResolves to
KnockbackWithFallbacksThe hit's knockback; if that is zero, attacker-to-target; if that is zero too, right.
AttackerToTargetAway from LastHit.attackerPosition; right when the two coincide.
UpStraight up.
FacingThe renderer's flipX — true means facing left, the convention four enemy states already share.
TowardCameraToward the frame's centre. The camera is orthographic in every scene, so there is no depth axis to point down; a body already dead centre gets up.

Position answers "where does this beat go", and is read by SpawnPrefab and by a destination-mode Translate. All five options resolve — which is new. ResolvePosition is to Position what Resolve is to Direction, and before it existed Spawn special-cased SelfPlusRandomInCircle and every other entry in the dropdown silently meant Self: a designer who picked GroundBelow got the body's own position and no way to find out. The same "granted but not wired" shape, in the same struct, twice.

PositionResolverResolves to
SelfThe body's own position. On a Translate this is also the mode switch: it keeps From/To meaning local offsets.
SelfPlusRandomInCircleThe body ± 0.5 units, re-rolled per spawn.
CameraCenterThe camera's x/y, at the body's own z.
ScreenEdgeThe frame edge the beat's Direction points at — the same geometry CombatUtils uses to place the off-screen blast, so a beat that FLINGS a body at the edge and a beat that DETONATES there cannot disagree about where the edge is. They did: the fling was a hard-coded offset and the blast was resolved.
GroundBelowA downward cast, up to 24 units, against LevelLayers.FallingMask — everything the player collides with going down, so "the floor" means the same thing to a finisher as it does to a jump. A miss falls back to the body's own position, never to the cast's end: a beat that travelled to the bottom of a bottomless pit would leave the frame while the payoff held the world for three seconds.
Why the mask comes from LevelLayers and not from a name. The first draft of that cast asked for a layer called Floors, and this project has no such layer. LayerMask.GetMask answers 0 for a name it does not know — so the cast would have found no ground, every GroundBelow beat would have fallen back to the body's position, and a slam would have looked like a beat that simply did not fire.

03Composing a sequence — the cursor, the caps and the terminal

The authored list is not what plays. ExecutionSequence.Scheduled.Schedule turns it into an ordered, timed, accessibility-resolved plan — a pure function of authored data, no scene and no clock — and that plan is what both the runner and every picture in the tools read.

FieldWhat the scheduler does with it
DurationHow long this beat runs, and how far it pushes the beats after it. 0 is legal and means instantaneous. A negative is authoring noise, not a request to run backwards — SafeDuration reads it as 0.
WithPreviousAnchor this beat where the previous one started instead of after it. The only way to express "shake with the spawn"; a purely sequential list cannot say it. Caveat under Reduced: a beat the tier drops is skipped without updating the anchor, so a WithPrevious beat whose authored neighbour was dropped anchors to the last surviving beat's start instead — the tier can silently move beats that survived. A known scheduler defect awaiting a fix; author around it by not hanging a WithPrevious beat off a droppable one (Jitter, Rotate, CameraPunch, a sorting-push SetRenderer).
StartAtExtra seconds on top of wherever this beat would otherwise begin. Negatives are clamped to 0.
UnscaledCount this beat on the unscaled clock. Mostly moot: while the payoff holds the world (§05) every beat is on real seconds already, and the flag only decides the case where the clock is not held.
A Translate in destination mode resolves its target once, when the beat starts (§02) — which is placement, not choreography: the beat you schedule at 1.4 s aims at where the floor, the frame edge or the camera centre was at 1.4 s. Now that the driver starts beats rather than awaiting them, two tweens genuinely run at once, so that target is a local of the running beat and not a field — a shared one would have each tween steering the other. At once is not the same as composed: two concurrent beats on the same channel (a Jitter over a Translate, two Translates) each write the transform absolutely from their own capture, so the later-started one wins the frame wholesale and the body snaps when it ends. Overlap beats on different channels — a scale with a translate composes; a translate with a translate fights.

The cursor that sequential beats land on is a running maximum, not the last beat's end. A parallel beat that outlasts its sequential neighbour still pushes what follows — otherwise beats would silently overlap.

The driver does one job: wait for each beat's authored slot and start it. ExecutionPayoffState.Run walks the plan on the unscaled clock and calls StartCoroutine(Fire(beat)) — it never awaits a beat. Each beat owns its own duration on its own clock, several overlap because they were authored to, and the terminal arrives when the schedule says it does, which is the same number the world-hold was sized from. So At means what the timeline pictures draw.

One keyword was the difference between the schedule being a contract and being a suggestion, and it is the best argument on this page for testing the two halves separately. The driver used to say yield return Fire(beat). Fire is a coroutine that runs for the beat's duration, so awaiting it made every beat wait for its predecessor to finish — and WithPrevious, the flag whose entire job is "start alongside your neighbour", produced no parallelism whatsoever. Meteor's opening block (a freeze, a flash, a material ramp, a camera punch and a dim, all authored at t=0) played one after another: as Meteor was authored at the time, a 4.31 s plan taking 6.04 s of wall clock against a cinematic hold sized from the plan, so the world came back to life seventy percent of the way through the finisher — on every payoff in the set, every time.

It survived the tests because the tests measured the SCHEDULER, which was always right, and of the runner only that beats happen and the enemy dies, which was also always true. Rule 3 in the one place the flag and the behaviour were most obviously the same thing: Schedule is a pure function and can be proved without a scene, but that the driver obeys it is a separate claim and needs its own test.
A pause moves the whole schedule forward rather than running behind the menu. Time.unscaledTime does not care that MenuState.Push zeroed the clock, so a player who paused half a second into a three-second finisher used to watch the rest of it happen behind the pause menu — every screen beat refused by ScreenFxLayer's own modal guard, the enemy dead by the time they resumed, the payoff they paused to look at simply gone. The wait loop now adds each paused frame to both the start time and the deadline, so a beat stays due exactly as long after its predecessor as it was authored to be. Fired beats and the hold itself pause too, as of 2026-08-31. Both used to be wrong and both were the same mistake in different files. The layer no longer Clears under a modal — it SUSPENDS, blanking the screen while keeping the state and handing back the paused time on resume, so a Title being read and a held ScreenDim survive the pause instead of vanishing for the rest of the payoff. And the hold re-arms itself: the driver checks every frame that the hold it claims is still live and re-takes it for the REMAINING plan length once the menu closes, because MenuState.Pop writes timeScale = 1 unconditionally and the manager's own timer counts straight through a pause. Self-healing rather than edge-triggered, deliberately: an edge that is missed once is missed forever.

The caps, and why each is a safety property

  • MaxBeats = 64. A sequence is authored data and a campaign can supply it, so the list length is a budget and rule 7 says a budget needs a cap. Beats past it are never scheduled.
  • MaxPayoffSeconds = 6 s. The clamp is on the schedule: a beat that would start past 6 s is dropped, not merely truncated mid-flight, and one that would run over the line has its duration cut to fit. CombatTimeManager.TriggerCinematicHold clamps the world-hold to the same constant, so the two cannot disagree.
  • ExecutionBeat.MaxRepeat = 12. SpawnPrefab's Count, capped because the design wants a payoff whose detonation count comes from a live status stack — a budget fed by runtime state is an unbounded loop unless something bounds it.
End is the loop guard, and a loop guard that depends on an author remembering it is not one. Every plan ends in exactly one End: the scheduler appends one if you did not author it, and drops everything after the one you did. A sequence that never reached a terminal would be an enemy frozen mid-finisher forever — physics off, execution flag set, never dying, never paying Gleam, still blocking the room's clear condition. Authoring your own End therefore only matters when you want the payoff to stop early — and if you do, know that an early End is not a hard cut today: tween beats already running (Translate, Scale, Rotate, Jitter, SetMaterial) are started as their own coroutines and are not stopped by the terminal, so one whose duration crosses your End keeps animating the corpse into the death state until it runs out. The globals are cleaned (Clear catches an overhanging Title or dim); the body's own tweens are not. Author beats to finish before your End until the code stops them for you.

The kill is a post-condition, not a beat. It lives in the runner's finally, so it happens whether the sequence completed, returned early, or never started — including the case where the config itself is missing. End authors WHEN; the runner guarantees THAT. That rule was learned from the bespoke dash payoff, which put the kill on its last line behind two early exits: one unassigned shader and the enemy logged an error and then stood there forever.

The terminal also gives back the three global things a payoff borrows, before the kill: the held clock (CancelHitstop), the camera (CameraFollow.CancelPunchEverywhere) and the screen (ScreenFxLayer.Clear). Every one of them is worse to leak than to never have taken — a leaked hold is a game stuck in slow motion, a leaked punch is a frame that never pulls back out, a leaked dim is a world that stays dark, and all three outlive the enemy that caused them and read as an engine bug. All three releases are now gated on being the LAST live payoff. The clock was gated first and the camera and screen were not, so under concurrent finishers (one arrow through two bodies) whichever terminal arrived first cleared the title, dim and punch the still-running payoff was relying on. A static list of live payoffs — a list rather than a counter, so an entry can be reclaimed by inspection when its machine is destroyed or merely deactivated under the coroutine — means the globals come back exactly once, when the last finisher ends.

04The traps — a payoff that schedules perfectly and renders nothing

Every failure below produces the same symptom: the beat list looks right, the schedule is happy, the enemy dies on time, and the player sees the world stop for a moment and the enemy vanish. Two debugging rounds went into exactly this, which is why the preview window prints the resolved schedule beside the picture (§07).

A Title has two deadlines, and both are invisible in the inspector. Its readable window is 0.6 × Duration — 14% fading in, 60% holding, 26% fading out — so the first pass at these ten, which averaged under half a second of hold, was the very rule-9 failure the kind was added to prevent: Vanishing Point, fifteen characters a player has never seen, was at full opacity for 0.24 s. Author ≥ 1.0 s, and longer for longer names. And a Title still fading when End arrives is cut mid-word, because the terminal calls ScreenFxLayer.Clear(): the name has to FINISH before the terminal, not merely start before it. Both are asserted over the shipped assets by ExecutionScheduleTests.
A strength channel MULTIPLIES its companion, so ramping one alone is N × nothing. _EmissionColor defaults to black and _OverlayTex defaults to "black". A SetMaterial beat that sweeps _EmissionStrength from 0 to 6 without an earlier beat setting _EmissionColor is multiplying six by black: it schedules correctly, runs for its full authored duration and renders no difference at all. That is how the first authored payoff shipped doing almost nothing.

The fix is a zero-length beat first. Set the companion, then ramp the strength — ExplosionPayoff, FirestormPayoff and OverloadPayoff all open their emission ramp with a Duration-0 _EmissionColor beat. Remember the branch rule from §02: a Tint is only read when the property name ends in Color, so the companion beat must name the colour channel, not the strength one. ExecutionScheduleTests.NoPayoffRampsAStrengthChannelWithoutItsCompanion sweeps every shipped asset for the unpaired shape, and the preview window reports it as a whole-payoff risk rather than a per-row one, because the fault lives between two beats.
An empty AnimationCurve is not null, and it evaluates to 0 forever. A serialized curve with no keys is a real object, so curve != null passes — and Evaluate on it answers 0 for every input, which pins a tweened beat at its start value for its whole duration. This is not an edge case: every unauthored curve field in every shipped asset serializes as m_Curve: [], and Unity deserializes that into exactly such an object. There is no such thing as a null curve on an asset.

The runner rescues it. ExecutionPayoffState.Shape checks curve.length > 0 as well as null and falls back to linear, so an untouched curve field eases linearly and is fine. What is not rescued is a curve that has keys and cannot move — a single keyframe, or two carrying the same value. It passes the length check, Evaluate answers the same number for every input, and the beat sits still while scheduling, timing and terminating perfectly. NoAuthoredPayoff_EasesATweenThroughACurveThatCannotMove sweeps the shipped assets for it, reading the rule back through Shape itself so the sweep tracks the runner rather than a copy of it.
GpuExplosion and SpriteSplit both need a Material and do nothing without one. They are the only two kinds that draw something which is not the enemy's own sprite — one passes the material's shader to the blast factory, the other instances it once per half so the slice shader can cull each side of the cut — and neither has a sane default, because a missing one is the difference between a finisher and an empty frame. So both are the exception to this vocabulary's usual silence: they log one error and skip rather than guess. The field points at a shared project asset and both readers clone before they write (rule 10); never assign it to a renderer directly. The two shipped materials are Assets/_Game/Combat/FX/FX_SpriteSlice.mat (used by SamuraiSlicePayoff's split) and FX_ProceduralExplosion.mat (used by OffScreenKillPayoff's screen-edge blast) — copy those onto a new beat rather than picking a material by name.
The quieter shapes, each of which the preview window names on its own row.
  • Scale with To at (0,0) — the runner reads that as "stay at From", so the beat holds the body still for its whole duration.
  • Translate (in Self mode) or Rotate with From equal to To — nothing travels, nothing turns. A destination-mode Translate travels to its resolved point whatever From/To say — Meteor's slam is authored (0,0) → (0,0) with GroundBelow and works — but the preview audit predates that mode and still flags it red; trust the mode, not the flag, until the audit is fixed.
  • Shake with To.y at 0, or Jitter with To.x at 0 — amplitude zero, nothing to move.
  • SetMaterial with From.x equal to To.x — the channel is held at one value for the beat's life.
  • CameraPunch at exactly ×1 — ApplyPunch returns before it takes the camera, which is correct and is also no punch.
  • SpawnPrefab with no prefab, Sound with no clip, SetMaterial with no property name — each waits out its slot and does nothing.
  • A whole payoff built from nothing but Freeze, Wait, SetPhysics and End — a legal, correctly scheduled sequence that shows the player a pause. EveryAuthoredPayoff_IsLongEnoughAndChangesSomethingOnScreen refuses it, under Reduced as well as Full, and holds a 1.5 s floor on the Full plan besides.
hitstopDuration on a payoff is inert ON PURPOSE, and must read as inert. The field is inherited from ExecutionStateConfig, where the three retired bespoke states each read it (all three deleted 2026-08-30); ExecutionPayoffState does not, and it is right not to. A payoff authors its hold as a Freeze beat, where the scheduler places it, the caps bound it and the accessibility tier clamps it — an implicit second hold applied by the config would sit outside all three, putting the one thing a motion-sensitive player most needs shortened outside the only mechanism that shortens it. The field cannot be removed from a subclass, so instead it is 0 on all ten payoff assets and NoAuthoredPayoff_SetsAHitstopTheRunnerNeverReads holds it there: a plausible number on an inspector row that changes nothing on screen is the shape of bug this project has the most of, and until 2026-08-29 every one of the ten authored one. And a Duration on an End beat is inert too, as §02 says. EnterSound does work — EnemyStateMachine plays any config's entry sound on state change, execution configs included.

05Accessibility — Full, Reduced, Off

The player has two rows and the game resolves them to one tier. The policy lives in the scheduler, not in the beats — so a beat implementation cannot execute a beat that has not been through it.

The rows are Settings ▸ Accessibility: Execution Cut-scenes (Full / Short / Off) and Reduce Motion (Off / On). GameSettings.ExecutionCinematicTier composes them by taking the stricter of the two — CinematicTier is ordered by severity (Full 0, Reduced 1, Off 2) so a Max over the pair is monotone and neither key can soften what the other asked for. Reduce Motion on its own reaches Reduced and stops there: "less motion" and "no finisher" are different promises. An out-of-range value in config.ini falls back to Full, deliberately — a stray number in a hand-editable text file must not be an accessibility bypass.

The menu says "Short" where the tier says "Reduced", on purpose. A player judges a finisher by how long it takes, which is the honest word on a menu row; the policy behind it also caps how far a beat goes, which is what "Reduced" names.

ExecutionPayoffState reads the tier once per execution, in Enter, through the TierSeam — the setting's only reader. Once, so that a player who opens the settings mid-finisher does not see the payoff change shape underneath them; through one seam, so that a beat added later cannot forget to ask, because it never asks at all.

TierWhat happens
FullThe shipped look. Reduce changes nothing and every beat survives.
ReducedDropped: Jitter, Rotate, CameraPunch, and a SetRenderer that pushes a sorting layer or order. Clamped: a Freeze to 0.1 s; a Scale to 1.4 per axis and a SetMaterial to 0.35, both at either end of the ramp rather than at the target only — the extreme is whichever end is larger, and a clamp that reads only the direction of travel does nothing for a shrink or a decaying flare. A SetMaterial naming a channel on ChannelIsLuminanceSafe is exempt entirely (§02). Re-shaped: a Shake keeps a decaying envelope with its amplitude capped at 0.35 — a shake that grows is the one shape that reads as motion rather than as impact. Everything else plays.
OffSubstitutes, it does not skip. The authored list is never consulted: the plan becomes a 0.1 s Freeze and an End. An empty plan would be the hang the terminal exists to prevent, so the kill still lands, the enemy still dies and the run does not desync. The runner also does not hold the clock under Off — somebody who turned cut-scenes off is asking for the game to keep moving.
The gate is a compile error, not a convention. The only way to obtain a Scheduled is from Schedule, whose constructor is private to it — which is why Schedule lives inside the struct rather than on the enclosing class. So a beat implementation cannot fabricate one, and therefore cannot execute a beat that has not been through the policy. The alternative — each implementation reading GameSettings — is one chance per implementation to forget, on a feature whose eleventh payoff will be authored by a campaign nobody here reviewed. Survives handles the kind nobody classified in the same spirit: a BeatKind with no explicit case is DROPPED under Reduced and named once per session in the console — the safe direction, because an effect nobody has classified is an effect nobody has decided is safe for a motion-sensitive player. Note that this is a warned fall-through, not a test failure: EveryBeatKindHasAPolicyInEveryTier only asserts the call does not throw, which reads identically for an explicit case and a fall-through.

Three gates the tier does not move

The tier decides which beats are scheduled and how far they go. Three of the beat implementations then pass through a second chokepoint that reads the player's settings directly, and a Full-tier plan does not override any of them:

  • CameraFollow.ApplyPunch returns immediately under Reduce Motion, whatever tier was resolved — and scales the departure from 1 by Screen Shake as Mathf.Lerp(1, scale, gate), never scale × gate. A punch is a multiplier around 1, not a magnitude around 0: naive multiplication of a 0.9 push-in by the Reduced gate of 0.5 yields 0.45, so the accessibility setting would make the effect it is meant to soften roughly five times stronger.
  • ShakeComponent.Shake multiplies by GameSettings.ScreenShakeScale (Full ×1, Reduced ×0.5, Off ×0), which is why a Shake beat must never write the camera transform itself.
  • ScreenFxLayer.Admit is the only method in that class that reads Reduce Flashing or spends the photosensitivity budget, and all five of its verbs — Flash, Wash, Letterbox, Dim, Title — go through it. Title passes raisesLuminance: false, so a name is neither clamped by Reduce Flashing nor spends the budget — words are information, not luminance. Every luminance-raising effect is rate-limited to one per 0.5 s for everybody — a refusal, not a queue, because a flash deferred until the budget reopens arrives detached from the beat that asked for it. Under Reduce Flashing the peak is clamped to 0.35, the life is lengthened to at least 0.25 s, and a 0.35 attack is added: what hurts is the transition rate, so the reduced form of a flash is a slow wash rather than a dim flash. The layer also refuses and clears everything while a modal is open, and holds a 6 s cap on any transient plus an 8 s budget on a held dim. It ships on GameServices.prefab — the persistent services root, so it outlives every scene — at sorting order 1, and it corrects a mis-set order at wake with a warning rather than silently. A beat still no-ops in a scene that has no layer, which is the sandbox case: it was in no scene or prefab until 2026-08-29, so every ScreenFlash and ScreenDim beat did nothing while every other signal said the payoff ran.
The world-hold has its own cap. While the payoff runs, TriggerCinematicHold takes the clock to ×0.05 for the plan's whole length and every beat animates on the unscaled clock underneath — a crawl rather than a stop, so particles and audio tails keep breathing. That is a different call from ordinary hitstop on purpose: TriggerDramaticFreeze runs every duration through the 2 s hitstop cap and the live feel knob, and a ~3 s finisher asked for 2.85 s, got 2 s, and the world came back to life three quarters of the way through its own cut-scene. The cinematic hold is capped at MaxPayoffSeconds instead, which is the same bound the scheduler already enforces. The separation guards both directions now. It used to guard one: TriggerHitstop refused to displace a live hold, but TriggerDramaticFreeze stopped whatever routine was running unconditionally — so a perfect hit landed during a finisher replaced the hold with a 2 s-capped freeze and the world ran at full speed under the rest of the choreography. A distinct cinematicActive flag now makes the dramatic freeze yield to a live hold exactly as ordinary hitstop yields to a dramatic freeze. A reward freeze inside a finisher is redundant anyway: the world is already stopped for the player to look at.

ScreenFxLayer also offers Wash and Letterbox. They ship, they are tested, and no beat kind drives either — so a payoff can flash the screen, dim it and name itself, and nothing more. Said out loud rather than left to be discovered, and tracked rather than only said: Documentation/OUTSTANDING.md, the row "Wash and Letterbox ship on the screen layer, and no beat kind drives either".

06Authoring one — the Workbench Executions tab

Gleamwood ▸ Content Workbench ▸ Executions. One page per payoff asset: identity and registration, the beat list, and the timeline the list actually resolves to under two tiers.

The cost of a fat struct is paid in the inspector — every field exists on every beat, so a raw list would show twenty fields of which a given kind reads three. A property drawer registered on ExecutionBeat is what makes that survivable, and because it travels with the struct rather than with a window it applies in the Workbench, in the Project-window inspector and in any surface added later. Each entry shows only the fields its Kind reads, with kind-specific labels — To means an amplitude to a Shake, a target scale to a Scale and a shader value to a SetMaterial.

  • Hidden is not deleted. A field the kind does not read is removed from the edit surface, but the value is still in the YAML — so a hidden field holding a non-default value is named on a red row with a confirmed one-click Clear. Behind a confirmation because switching Kind to look at something is a normal thing to do, and in that moment every field the real kind reads is "unread".
  • The field table is total over the enum — a row for all nineteen kinds — and a kind that ever loses one shows every field plus a red row naming it, rather than quietly showing none. Hiding a field the tool does not understand is the one failure a filtering drawer must never have, and the Workbench prints any missing kinds under the beat list so the gap can never be silent. Total is not the same as CURRENT, and this table has lagged the runner twice. The second time was the expensive one: the Translate row did not list Position, so the drawer HID the destination-mode field Meteor's slam is authored in, reported it as authored-but-unread, and offered a working control that would have cleared it — one confirmed click from deleting a shipped finisher's headline beat, with a green suite. Both rows were corrected on 2026-08-31 along with SpawnPrefab's stale claim that three of the five Position options fall back to Self. When in doubt, §02's table is read off the runner and is the one to trust.
  • The timeline cannot lie. It never re-derives the schedule; it calls ExecutionSequence.Scheduled.Schedule — the same pure function the runner calls — and draws what comes back. It maps authored beats to their slots by scheduling prefixes of the list rather than by copying the placement rules, which is exact because Schedule is a left fold. A row marked * was clamped rather than dropped; hover it for the before and after, because a clamp changes the beat without moving its bar.
  • Two columns, one axis. Full and Reduced are drawn against a shared maximum, or a shorter Reduced plan would be drawn the same width and the accessibility difference — the whole reason they sit side by side — would be invisible. Off is not drawn at all, because it never reads the list.
  • Sweep all execution payoff assets catches the two things a per-asset page structurally cannot see: a StateName shared with another execution state (registration dedupes, so only one of them is ever reachable) and beats carrying values their kind never reads.

07Watching one — the preview window

Gleamwood ▸ Execution Preview… plays any authored payoff on any shipped enemy at any tier, on demand. The only other way to see a finisher is to earn it — buy a particular weapon, reach an enemy that offers that execution, fill its meter and land a specific hit with a specific reaction — and a payoff nobody can watch is a payoff nobody can tune.

Pick a payoff on the left (the list is swept from the project, so a tenth and a campaign's eleventh both appear with no edit), pick a subject — ★ marks an enemy whose definition actually offers it in a run, but any enemy can be driven into any payoff, which is what makes the window useful before the ExecutionDefinition exists — choose a tier, and press Play. In play mode it spawns immediately; from edit mode it enters play mode and picks the request up on the other side of the domain reload. Replay at this tier pushes the current selection onto the live driver first, so changing the tier and pressing Replay is a one-click A/B of the accessibility policy.

The readout beside the picture is the load-bearing half. It runs the real Schedule and prints what it returns, row by row, with at and for against each beat. That answers the one question a preview raises and a static inspector cannot: "it played, so why is nothing on screen?" — if a beat is listed with a time against it, it RAN, so the fault is in what that beat draws, not in whether the preview fired. Two debugging rounds were spent on payoffs that scheduled perfectly and rendered nothing (an empty curve, a strength channel over a black companion) and both look identical on screen to "the preview did not fire". Each row also carries a plain-English description and, where it applies, a ⚠ naming the reason that beat will render nothing; whole-payoff risks that live between beats are listed underneath. Copy schedule to clipboard gives the same rows as text, for a bug report or to diff two tiers.
  • It never writes an asset, and never writes your settings. The subject is instantiated by value so it carries no prefab connection, and the tier is overridden through ExecutionPayoffState.TierSeam rather than by writing GameSettings — a preview tool that edits the person's own settings is a preview tool that changes their game.
  • It prints the three gates it cannot preview away — this editor's Reduce Motion, Screen Shake and Reduce Flashing, and what they would resolve to — because a Full-tier preview on an editor with Reduce Motion on plays no camera punch at all, and without that row it reads exactly like a broken CameraPunch beat.
  • Two preflight checks against the assets, rather than discovered at play time: a blank StateName (the preview falls back to the asset name; a run would not), and an enemy whose definition has no dead state. The second is a warning, not a failure: the terminal uses TryGetState since 2026-08-30, so a missing death state no longer throws — IsDead is still set, the enemy just plays no death animation. The window's warning says exactly that now — it described the old throw-and-go-red behaviour until 2026-08-31.
  • It plays on a dedicated stage, not in the scene you have open. Assets/_Scenes/ExecutionTest.unity — a fixed orthographic camera at base zoom, a global light, a floor on the Level layer, a CombatTimeManager and the fullscreen effect layer, and nothing else. That removes the variable: previewing in place meant the same finisher looked different in the Hub and in a biome, because the lighting, the zoom and the backdrop were whatever happened to be loaded. It also removes six objects of runtime setup per preview, which on a ten-payoff walk is sixty. Exiting play mode returns you to the scene you had open — nothing is written to it.

    The toggle Play on the ExecutionTest stage turns it off, and that is worth doing deliberately: a finisher against a real room, with that room's light and clutter, is a different and occasionally necessary question. Off, the driver falls back to standing up a camera, clock and effect layer wherever the open scene has none — and says in the console which pieces the scene was missing, because a rig quietly assembled at runtime is a rig nobody knows is there. The driver builds the light too: when the open scene has no Light2D at all it stands up a white global one at intensity 1 — the same recipe ExecutionTestStage.Build writes into the stage — and reports it among the pieces it created. A scene that already has any Light2D keeps its own lighting untouched, because previewing against a real room is a supported thing to want. See the light bullet below. A scene that immediately loads another one (0.Bootstrapper) still takes an off-stage preview away with it.
  • The stage is GENERATED, and a lint keeps it honest. Gleamwood ▸ Execution Preview ▸ Rebuild Test Stage rebuilds it from ExecutionTestStage, so a stage someone has broken is restored from a menu item rather than from git. What it must contain is declared once in ExecutionStage.Required — each entry naming the component and what goes silent without it — and ExecutionScheduleTests sweeps the saved scene for every one of them. The lint reads the scene's YAML rather than loading it: a single-mode scene load takes the scene the loader lives in, which in a fixture is the test runner's own. It matches script GUIDs, not type names, because that is how a scene stores a component — grepping a scene for ScreenFxLayer finds nothing and proves nothing, which is exactly how that layer came to be referenced by no scene or prefab at all.
  • The light is not decoration. 2D URP renders an unlit scene black, so a stage without a global light shows a perfectly correct payoff as nothing at all — the same class of failure as a strength channel over a black companion, and just as convincing. It is the first thing to check if a preview goes dark after someone edits the stage.
  • It drives the payoff the way the game does — ChangeState(targetState), the same call TakeHit makes, with the beats running through ExecutionPayoffState off the same asset a run would load. Rule 2 in its usual clothing: a payoff that renders nothing here renders nothing there.
Why an execution did not fire is a different question, and it has its own tool: F1 ▸ Run (dev) ▸ Execution gates ▸ Explain executions records, per hit, which gate closed on each definition and why — see combat.html §07. This window is for a payoff that fired and looked wrong.

08Worked example — Meteor, beat by beat

MeteorPayoff.asset, seventeen authored beats resolving to 2.98 s — under Full and under Reduced, because the beat that sets the opening block's length (the 1.02 s Title) survives both tiers. Earned by launching a target and landing an aerial melee on it — the airborne catch-all, since Skyhook sits above it and claims the Spike variant (§01). It is the clearest shape in the set because its three movements are obvious: the hit lands, the frame closes in and holds, the body is driven into the floor.

What Reduced does to it, beat by beat: the camera punch (3) is dropped outright; the freeze (0) is clamped from 0.18 to 0.1 s; both Scales (8, 13) lose their 1.5 to the 1.4 cap — beat 13 on its From, which is the half a To-only clamp used to miss; the shake (10) drops from amplitude 0.85 to 0.35; and both _FlashAmount sweeps (2, 12) have their From pulled from 1 to 0.35, so the white-out opens dim instead of opening bright and being clamped to a value it was already heading for. The Title (5) survives with its curve cleared — the words stay, only the choreographed entrance goes, because an accessibility setting must not cost the player what the game is telling them. The dissolve (15) is untouched, because _DissolveAmount is on the luminance-safe list — a fade that stopped at 0.35 would leave a body two-thirds solid, which is a bug rather than a kindness. Sixteen beats instead of seventeen, and the same 2.98 s length — the punch was the only drop, and the surviving 1.02 s Title sets the opening block's cursor in both tiers — and the payoff still reads, because the fall, the squash-and-stretch, the spawn and the full dissolve all survive the tier. That is the bar EveryAuthoredPayoff_IsLongEnoughAndChangesSomethingOnScreen holds: a payoff whose only visible content is a spin or a punch renders as a pause for exactly the players least likely to report it as a bug.

The slam was a fixed −3 on Y until 2026-08-30 — it punched through a floor two units down and stopped in mid-air over one six units down. It is the GroundBelow destination its design brief asked for now, and the zero offset is exact rather than tuned: every enemy's colliders extend upward from the transform origin, so the pivot is at the feet and landing the pivot on the cast's hit point lands the feet on the surface. A quirk worth knowing until the tools catch up: because the beat's From and To are both (0,0), the preview window's per-row audit currently flags it "From equals To — nothing travels" — a stale check that predates destination mode; the beat travels.

The nine other payoffs are worth reading beside it; nine of the ten sit between 2.5 and 3.0 s under Full, with SkyhookPayoff the outlier at 2.00 s. ExplosionPayoff and NailedPayoff are the busiest — 32 beats each, a repeating detonation figure. SamuraiSlicePayoff is the sparsest at 13 beats, hanging a 1.7 s SpriteSplit and a camera punch off one anchor — and its _EmissionStrength runs 2.2 down to 0, the decaying-flare shape the both-ends clamp exists for. PinfallPayoff is the quietest on screen: a slam, a dim, then three Sound beats ~0.48 s apart, a referee's count told entirely in audio (the spacing tightened from 0.66 s to buy its Title room under the length caps). OverloadPayoff is the one to copy for a charge-up, its 1.55 s _EmissionStrength ramp correctly primed by a zero-length _EmissionColor beat first (§04). And OffScreenKillPayoff shows the other material-bearing kind: it flings the body out through the screen edge its AttackerToTarget direction resolves — the fling Translate is authored at the ScreenEdge resolver with a To offset of (0, 3.2), so the throw follows the hit — then fires a real GpuExplosion at the screen edge its AttackerToTarget direction resolves, with a shake, a sound and a flash on the same anchor. Both halves read the same direction — and until 2026-08-31 they did not. The fling was authored in Self mode as a fixed 9.6-unit offset up and to the right, so on every leftward kill the body flew right while the blast detonated left. Fixed by moving the fling onto the resolver the blast already used, which is what the ScreenEdge position resolver exists for (§02).

The reservations that keep the ten distinct are enforced by tests, not by memory. Growth on both axes past 1.4 and any SortingOrder push are Skyhook's alone; GpuExplosion is OffScreenKill's alone; the sprite split is SamuraiSlice's alone — Meteor, Pinfall, Groundbreaker and Firestorm are all sword kills and must never halve the sprite, or the melee set swallows the dash signature. On both axes is a correction the lint forced: Meteor scales (0.72, 1.5), which is squash-and-stretch with the footprint unchanged, and the original wording ("nothing else may scale past 1.4") would have outlawed the most basic impact language in 2D animation. The highest-risk cluster is Firestorm / Explosion / Overload, separated on three axes at once: count (continuous / staccato / one), direction (up / outward / inward-then-out), and colour (orange consuming / orange bursting / white-cyan discharge).
